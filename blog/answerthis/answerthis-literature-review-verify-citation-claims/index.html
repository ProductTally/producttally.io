How to verify a hybrid-work claim in an AnswerThis literature review

Check original papers, samples and outcomes before citing research for a claim about hybrid work.

By Evan · Updated Nov 18, 2026

Check each cited paper for the outcome it actually measured. AnswerThis has tools for literature reviews and citation checks. Use those to organize candidates, then open the papers and check the sample, intervention and result. Two citations after one sentence can answer different questions.

Find the paper behind the citation

  • AnswerThis research tools

In AnswerThis, open Literature Review and select research papers as a source. Ask which studies tested hybrid work and report the outcome for each paper. Use the linked citations to open the original sources. If you select model-only mode, switch to a source-backed search before citing the result.

  • AnswerThis Literature Review guide

What did the two Chinese company studies measure?

Consider the claim “Several trials found that working from home two days a week raises productivity.” A 2024 randomized Trip.com trial published in Nature involved 1,612 employees in roles including engineering, marketing and finance. It tested two home days per week. The authors found lower attrition and no evidence of worse performance. The productivity increase in the draft would need its own evidence.

  • Trip.com randomized trial

In the published results, the share of employees who left was 7.2% in the office group and 4.8% in the hybrid group, a 2.4 percentage-point difference. The paper separately examines performance grades. Keep the numbers attached to attrition when you cite them.

An earlier Ctrip randomized trial involved call-center staff with a different home-working schedule. It reported improved performance for that setup. The roles, schedule and measures differ. Use the study to discuss how remote arrangements vary by job, with its original conditions attached.

  • Ctrip call-center study

Rewrite the sentence after you check the papers

For each citation, record whether it points to an original study, preprint or someone else's summary. Compare DOIs and study details so you don't count two versions of one trial as two trials. Record who was studied, the comparison groups, what changed and the result. Here you could write: “A randomized trial in selected Trip.com departments found that two days at home per week improved retention, with no evidence of worse performance.” Discuss the call-center study separately. The source findings were checked by hand, and the example does not measure the product's accuracy.
